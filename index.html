Write definition + points + example
01
Explain the origin, meaning, scope and features of cybercrime.
Paper 1 · Q16(a) · also supports “Define cybercrime”
Definition: Cybercrime is an illegal activity in which a computer, mobile device, network or the internet is used as a tool, target or place of crime.
  1. Origin: Cybercrime grew as computers, networks and internet services became widely used.
  2. Meaning: It includes unauthorized access, stealing data, online fraud and damaging digital systems.
  3. Against individuals: Identity theft, cyberstalking, harassment and financial fraud.
  4. Against property: Software piracy, data theft and damage to digital assets.
  5. Against organizations: Hacking, data breaches, ransomware and denial-of-service attacks.
  6. Against society or government: Cyberterrorism, online forgery and attacks on public services.
  7. Main features: Can be committed remotely, quickly and sometimes anonymously; evidence is digital and may be difficult to trace.
  8. Example: A criminal sends a fake bank link and steals a victim’s login details.
Conclusion: Cybercrime affects people, businesses and governments, so awareness, security controls and timely reporting are essential.
02
Classify cybercrimes and give examples.
Paper 1 · Q16(b)
Definition: Cybercrimes can be classified according to the target affected by the crime.
  1. Against individuals: Phishing, cyberstalking, identity theft, online harassment and banking fraud.
  2. Against property: Software piracy, intellectual-property theft, data destruction and internet-time theft.
  3. Against organizations: Hacking, password sniffing, email bombing, ransomware and DoS/DDoS attacks.
  4. Against society: Online forgery, spreading illegal content, cyberterrorism and website hijacking.
  5. Against government: Attacks on government websites, theft of confidential information and disruption of critical services.
  6. Active attacks: Change or disrupt data; examples are DDoS and website defacement.
  7. Passive attacks: Secretly observe information; examples are eavesdropping and packet sniffing.
Conclusion: Classification helps investigators understand the target, impact and suitable response.
03
Explain how cybercriminals plan cyberattacks.
Paper 1 · Q12(a) · supports cyberattack case handling
Definition: Cyberattack planning is the process criminals use to select a target, find weaknesses and attempt an illegal activity.
  1. Target selection: Choose an individual, company, bank or government system.
  2. Reconnaissance: Collect public information such as email addresses, job roles and website details.
  3. Find weaknesses: Look for weak passwords, outdated software or careless security practices.
  4. Select a method: Choose phishing, malware, password attacks or exploitation of a vulnerability.
  5. Create a trap: Use a fake email, website, message or attachment to gain trust.
  6. Launch the attack: Attempt to steal credentials, access data or interrupt services.
  7. Hide evidence: Criminals may try to disguise their identity or remove traces.
  8. Gain benefit: They may seek money, data, control or revenge.
Example: A fake delivery SMS directs a victim to a copied website that collects login details. Awareness and layered security help stop such attacks.
04
Explain social engineering and ways to prevent it in an organization.
Paper 1 · Q17(b) · Unit II notes
Definition: Social engineering is the use of deception or persuasion to make people reveal confidential information or perform unsafe actions.
  1. Impersonation: An attacker pretends to be an employee, technician or trusted person.
  2. Phishing: Fake emails or websites trick users into sharing information.
  3. Pretexting: A false story is used to gain trust.
  4. Baiting: A tempting offer or infected USB is used to attract a victim.
  5. Urgency and fear: Messages pressure people to act without checking.
  6. Training: Teach staff to identify suspicious requests and verify identities.
  7. Verification: Confirm payment changes and sensitive requests using a second trusted channel.
  8. Technical controls: Use MFA, spam filters, least-privilege access and clear reporting procedures.
Conclusion: People are an important part of security. Training plus technical controls reduces social-engineering risk.
05
Explain cybercafes and their connection with cybercrime.
Paper 1 · Q12(b)
Definition: A cybercafe is a public place where people pay to use computers and internet access.
  1. Users share computers and network connections.
  2. Criminals may misuse public access to try to hide their identity.
  3. Shared computers can retain browser history, downloaded files or saved passwords.
  4. Weak monitoring may allow illegal activity to go unnoticed.
  5. Other risks include shoulder surfing, malware and theft of personal information.
  6. Cybercafes should maintain lawful user records as required, keep systems updated and use security software.
  7. Users should log out, avoid saving passwords and never access sensitive accounts on untrusted devices.
Conclusion: Public internet access is useful, but responsible monitoring and safe user behaviour are important.

Phishing, identity theft & online identity

Repeated phishing and identity-theft questions are combined here

06
Explain email phishing, smishing and vishing. How can they be prevented?
Paper 2 · Q16(a) · Paper 1 · Q18
Definition: Phishing is a social-engineering attack that tricks people into revealing information or opening harmful links.
  1. Email phishing: Fake emails imitate banks, companies or services and contain malicious links or attachments.
  2. Smishing: Phishing through SMS or text messages, often using fake delivery or bank alerts.
  3. Vishing: Phishing through voice calls, where a caller pretends to be bank staff or technical support.
  4. Why effective: Messages use urgency, fear, rewards or authority to make people act quickly.
  5. Possible impact: Stolen passwords, financial loss, identity theft, malware infection and account takeover.
  6. Check the sender: Inspect the full email address and verify the message through an official app or website.
  7. Avoid unsafe actions: Do not click unexpected links, open unknown attachments or share OTPs/PINs.
  8. Report and protect: Report suspicious messages, use spam filters, MFA and updated security software.
Conclusion: Verify every unexpected request independently before clicking or sharing information.
07
Compare traditional phishing with spear phishing and spy-phishing.
Paper 2 · Q16(b) · Q11 phishing alternatives
Definition: Phishing uses deception to steal information. Spear phishing is targeted, while “spy-phishing” in the paper refers to phishing designed to capture or monitor a victim’s information.
  1. Traditional phishing: A broad message is sent to many people, such as a fake bank email.
  2. Spear phishing: A specific person or organization is targeted using personal details, job roles or familiar names.
  3. Spy-phishing: A deceptive link or copied login page is used to capture credentials or other sensitive information.
  4. Personalization: Traditional phishing is usually generic; spear phishing looks more relevant and believable.
  5. Scale: Traditional phishing often targets many users; spear phishing targets selected victims.
  6. Example: A generic “verify your account” email is traditional phishing; a tailored email naming an employee’s manager is spear phishing.
  7. Protection: Verify sender identity, inspect URLs, use MFA and report suspicious requests.
Conclusion: Targeted phishing can be harder to recognize, so users must verify even messages that appear personal.
08
List types of phishing scams and methods used in phishing attacks.
Paper 2 · Q11(a)/(b)
Definition: A phishing scam is a fake communication intended to trick a victim into sharing data, sending money or installing malware.
  1. Email phishing: Fake mass emails containing links or attachments.
  2. Spear phishing: Targeted messages for a particular person or organization.
  3. Whaling: Phishing aimed at senior executives or other high-level officials.
  4. Smishing: Fraudulent SMS or text messages.
  5. Vishing: Fraudulent phone calls or voice messages.
  6. Clone phishing: A genuine-looking message is copied and its link or attachment is replaced with a malicious one.
  7. Fake websites and ads: Copies of trusted sites collect passwords or payment information.
  8. Social media scams: Fake profiles, messages or giveaways collect personal information.
Prevention: Check the sender and website address, avoid unexpected attachments, use MFA and report suspicious messages.
09
Explain identity theft, its types and prevention.
Paper 1 · Q14(b) · Paper 2 · Q18(b)
Definition: Identity theft occurs when someone obtains and uses another person’s personal information without permission, usually for fraud.
  1. Financial identity theft: Stolen bank or card details are used for purchases or withdrawals.
  2. Criminal identity theft: A person uses another person’s identity to avoid responsibility.
  3. Medical identity theft: Someone misuses another person’s identity to obtain medical services.
  4. Child identity theft: A child’s information is used to open accounts or obtain services.
  5. Synthetic identity theft: Real and invented details are combined to create a false identity.
  6. Protect information: Share personal details only with trusted services and limit public social-media information.
  7. Secure accounts: Use unique passwords, MFA, device updates and account alerts.
  8. Respond quickly: Contact the bank/service provider, change affected passwords, preserve evidence and report the incident.
Conclusion: Careful sharing, strong authentication and fast reporting help reduce identity theft.
10
Explain steps to efface your online identity or reduce your digital footprint.
Paper 1 · Q19(a) · Paper 2 · Q18(a)
Definition: A digital footprint is the trail of information a person leaves through online activity. Effacing it means reducing unnecessary personal information online; complete removal may not be possible.
  1. Audit accounts: Search your name and list old social-media profiles, websites and apps.
  2. Delete unused accounts: Use the official account-deletion process where possible.
  3. Remove old posts: Delete unnecessary personal posts, photos and public details.
  4. Adjust privacy: Make profiles private and restrict who can see posts and contact information.
  5. Request removal: Contact website owners or platforms to remove information where allowed.
  6. Limit permissions: Revoke unnecessary app access and disable location sharing when not needed.
  7. Secure identity: Change reused passwords, enable MFA and monitor account activity.
  8. Be careful going forward: Avoid posting addresses, phone numbers, identity documents or travel details publicly.
Conclusion: Regular account reviews and careful sharing reduce exposure, but copies and legal records may remain online.

Passwords, spyware & malware

Definitions, differences, detection and countermeasures

11
Explain common password-cracking methods and distinguish cracking from guessing.
Paper 2 · Q15(a) · Q17(a)
Definition: Password cracking is the attempt to recover or discover a password. Password guessing is trying likely passwords, often based on common words or personal details.
  1. Brute-force attack: Tries many or all possible character combinations.
  2. Dictionary attack: Tries words and common passwords from a prepared list.
  3. Password guessing: Uses likely choices such as names or common patterns.
  4. Credential stuffing: Tries leaked username-password pairs on other services.
  5. Password spraying: Tries a small number of common passwords across many accounts.
  6. Phishing/social engineering: Tricks users into revealing their password.
  7. Keylogging: Malware or hardware records keys typed by the user.
  8. Prevention: Use long unique passwords, a password manager, MFA, login-rate limits and breach monitoring.
Difference: Guessing usually tests likely choices; cracking is the broader term and can include automated hash recovery or systematic attempts. Example: trying “Welcome123” is guessing; testing many combinations is brute-force cracking.
12
Develop guidelines for creating secure passwords for an administrator system.
Paper 2 · Q14(b) · Paper 1 · Q15(b)
Definition: A secure password is difficult to guess and is used only for its intended account.
  1. Use a long passphrase; length is more important than a predictable pattern.
  2. Use a unique password for every account, especially administrator accounts.
  3. Avoid names, birthdays, phone numbers and common words or sequences.
  4. Use a trusted password manager to generate and store passwords.
  5. Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA).
  6. Never share passwords or send them in plain text.
  7. Change a password immediately if it is exposed or suspected to be compromised.
  8. Limit administrator access, use separate admin accounts and monitor failed login attempts.
Conclusion: Strong unique passwords plus MFA and access controls greatly reduce unauthorized access.
13
Explain keyloggers and their working. How can they be prevented?
Paper 2 · Q12(b)
Definition: A keylogger is software or hardware that records the keys a user types.
  1. Types: Software keyloggers run on a device; hardware keyloggers are physical devices connected to a keyboard or computer.
  2. Infection/placement: A software keylogger may arrive through malicious downloads, attachments or compromised software.
  3. Recording: It captures keystrokes, potentially including usernames, passwords and messages.
  4. Storage/transmission: Recorded data may be saved locally or sent to an attacker.
  5. Misuse: Criminals may use the information for account takeover or financial fraud.
  6. Detection: Use updated security software, inspect unfamiliar programs and check unusual device connections.
  7. Prevention: Keep systems updated, download only from trusted sources, use MFA and avoid suspicious attachments.
  8. Response: Disconnect a suspected infected device from networks, use trusted security support and change passwords from a clean device.
Conclusion: Preventing malware and using MFA can reduce the damage caused by keyloggers.
14
Differentiate between a computer virus and a worm.
Paper 2 · Q12(a)
Definition: A virus attaches to a host file or program; a worm is standalone malware that can spread automatically across systems or networks.
  1. Host: A virus usually needs a host file; a worm does not need to attach to another file.
  2. Spread: A virus commonly spreads when an infected file runs or is shared; a worm can self-replicate across networks.
  3. User action: Viruses often depend on a user running an infected file; worms may spread without direct user action.
  4. Speed: Worms can spread rapidly over vulnerable networks.
  5. Impact: Both may corrupt data, slow systems or deliver other malware.
  6. Prevention: Update systems, use security software, avoid unknown files and maintain backups.
Example: An infected document may carry a virus; a network worm may copy itself between vulnerable computers.
15
Explain the risks of Trojan horses and backdoors. How can they be detected?
Paper 2 · Q13(a) · Q17(b)
Definition: A Trojan horse is malicious software disguised as legitimate software. A backdoor is a hidden method of bypassing normal authentication or security controls.
  1. Unauthorized access: Attackers may enter a system without normal login checks.
  2. Data theft: Files, credentials and confidential information may be stolen.
  3. Remote control: An infected system may be controlled remotely.
  4. Further malware: A backdoor may allow additional malicious software to be installed.
  5. Detection signs: Unknown processes, unexpected network connections, changed settings or unusual system behaviour.
  6. Security scans: Use updated endpoint protection and trusted malware scans.
  7. System checks: Review startup items, user accounts, logs and network activity.
  8. Response: Isolate the device, preserve evidence, remove the threat safely, patch systems and reset credentials from a clean device.
Conclusion: Download software only from trusted sources and monitor systems for unusual activity.
16
Summarize spyware and explain how to prevent spyware infections.
Paper 2 · Q5 MCQ expanded · Paper 1 · Q19(b)
Definition: Spyware is software that secretly monitors user activity or collects information without proper permission.
  1. It may monitor browsing, collect personal data or track user behaviour.
  2. Some spyware records keystrokes or captures login information.
  3. It can arrive through suspicious downloads, fake updates, malicious links or bundled software.
  4. Symptoms may include unusual pop-ups, unknown applications, browser changes or unexplained network activity.
  5. Keep the operating system, browser and applications updated.
  6. Download apps only from trusted sources and review installation permissions.
  7. Use reputable security software and enable firewall protections.
  8. If infected, disconnect if necessary, scan with trusted tools and change important passwords from a clean device.
Conclusion: Safe downloading, updates and monitoring are the main defences against spyware.
17
Explain steganography and how it differs from encryption.
Paper 2 · Q13(b)
Definition: Steganography hides the existence of a message inside another file, such as an image, audio or video file.
  1. Purpose: Make the presence of secret information difficult to notice.
  2. Cover file: An ordinary image, audio file or document is selected.
  3. Embedding: Secret data is inserted into the cover file using a steganographic method.
  4. Transmission: The file is shared so that it appears ordinary.
  5. Extraction: The receiver uses the appropriate method or key to retrieve the hidden data.
  6. Encryption: Changes readable data into unreadable ciphertext to hide its meaning.
  7. Main difference: Steganography hides that a message exists; encryption hides what the message says.
  8. Combined use: A message can be encrypted first and then hidden for added protection.
Example: Encryption makes a message unreadable, while steganography may hide it inside an image. Steganography is not automatically secure if the hidden data is discovered.

Cybercrime investigation & computer forensics

Investigation workflow, evidence integrity, reporting and network detection

18
Explain the main steps involved in investigating a cybercrime.
Paper 2 · Q20(b) · Paper 1 · Q15(a)
Definition: Cybercrime investigation is the systematic process of identifying, collecting, examining and presenting digital evidence to understand a crime and support legal action.
  1. Identification: Detect the incident and identify possible evidence sources.
  2. Preservation: Protect devices and data from alteration; document the scene and maintain chain of custody.
  3. Collection: Collect logs, emails, files, storage media and network records using approved methods.
  4. Forensic imaging: Make a bit-by-bit copy where appropriate and work on the copy rather than the original.
  5. Examination: Recover relevant files and identify suspicious activity or malware.
  6. Analysis: Correlate evidence and reconstruct the sequence of events.
  7. Documentation: Record tools, methods, dates, findings and evidence handling.
  8. Presentation: Prepare a clear report and present legally valid evidence to the appropriate authority or court.
Conclusion: Proper evidence handling and documentation protect the integrity and credibility of an investigation.
19
Explain the importance of computer forensics in cybercrime investigation.
Paper 1 · Q20(a)
Definition: Computer forensics is the identification, preservation, examination, analysis and presentation of computer-based digital evidence in a reliable, legally acceptable manner.
  1. Identify evidence: Locate relevant computers, storage devices, emails, logs and files.
  2. Preserve integrity: Protect original evidence and prevent accidental changes.
  3. Recover data: Find deleted, hidden or relevant files when possible.
  4. Trace activity: Examine timestamps, logs and file history to understand what happened.
  5. Find attack methods: Identify malware, unauthorized access or suspicious communication.
  6. Build a timeline: Connect events to reconstruct the incident.
  7. Support legal action: Document how evidence was collected and handled.
  8. Prevent future incidents: Findings can reveal weaknesses that need to be fixed.

Common tools: EnCase, FTK, Autopsy, Wireshark and Cellebrite UFED, depending on the evidence type.

Conclusion: Computer forensics turns digital traces into carefully documented evidence for investigation and legal proceedings.
20
How can investigators identify a cyberattack in a network?
Paper 2 · Q19(b)
Definition: Network attack identification is the process of examining network activity and system records to detect suspicious or unauthorized actions.
  1. Monitor traffic: Review network flows, firewall logs and intrusion-detection alerts.
  2. Look for unusual patterns: Large traffic spikes, repeated connection failures or unknown destinations.
  3. Check endpoints: Inspect affected computers for suspicious processes, files and account changes.
  4. Review authentication logs: Look for repeated failed logins, unfamiliar locations or unexpected privileges.
  5. Compare timestamps: Correlate network, server and endpoint events into a timeline.
  6. Identify indicators: Record suspicious IP addresses, domains, file hashes and other relevant indicators.
  7. Contain safely: Isolate affected systems when needed while preserving evidence.
  8. Document and recover: Report findings, remove the cause, patch weaknesses and monitor for recurrence.
Conclusion: Combining logs, traffic analysis and endpoint evidence gives a clearer picture than relying on one alert alone.
21
Why should cybercrime be reported promptly? Give its benefits.
Paper 2 · Q19(a) · Paper 1 · Q10 MCQ
Definition: Cybercrime reporting means informing the appropriate authorities or service providers about an online crime and supplying available evidence.
  1. Limit damage: Quick reporting may help block fraudulent transactions or stop ongoing access.
  2. Improve recovery: Banks and service providers may act faster when notified early.
  3. Preserve evidence: Logs, messages and account records may be easier to obtain before they disappear.
  4. Identify offenders: Authorities can investigate and connect related incidents.
  5. Protect others: Reports help warn other users and improve security measures.
  6. Support legal action: Official records can support an investigation and prosecution.
  7. Keep proof: Save screenshots, transaction IDs, emails, URLs and timestamps; do not alter the evidence.
  8. Act safely: Contact the relevant platform/bank and official cybercrime reporting channels promptly.
Conclusion: Reporting early improves the chance of limiting harm, preserving evidence and preventing similar crimes.

India’s cyber security initiatives & recent attacks

Use a clear definition, key functions and an example of impact

22
Explain cyber security initiatives in India and their effectiveness.
Paper 1 · Q20(b) · Paper 2 · Q14(a)/(b)
Definition: Cybersecurity initiatives are government programmes, agencies and rules that help prevent, detect and respond to cyber threats.
  1. CERT-In: India’s national agency for responding to cybersecurity incidents; it issues alerts, advisories and incident-handling guidance.
  2. MeitY: The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology supports national digital and cybersecurity policy.
  3. Cybercrime reporting: The national cybercrime reporting portal helps people report online crimes; financial fraud victims should report immediately through official channels.
  4. Cyber Swachhta Kendra: Supports detection and removal of botnet/malware threats and promotes cyber hygiene.
  5. IT Act, 2000: Provides a legal framework for electronic records and several cyber offences and penalties.
  6. Awareness programmes: Educate people about phishing, passwords, privacy and safe internet use.
  7. Effectiveness: These measures improve incident reporting, awareness, coordination and response.
  8. Challenges: Attacks change quickly; under-reporting, lack of awareness and skills gaps remain challenges.
Conclusion: Initiatives are important, but their success also depends on timely reporting, trained staff, updated systems and public awareness.
23
Explain two examples of recent cyber security attacks and their impact.
Paper 2 · Q20(a)
Definition: A cybersecurity attack is an attempt to access, damage, disrupt or steal data from a computer system or network.
  1. Ransomware attack: Attackers encrypt an organization’s files and demand payment. It can stop hospital, business or public services, cause financial loss and expose data.
  2. DDoS attack: Attackers flood a server or service with traffic so legitimate users cannot access it. It can cause downtime, lost revenue and reduced public trust.
  3. Phishing-led data breach: A victim is tricked into sharing credentials, allowing attackers to access email or company systems.
  4. Common impacts: Financial loss, service disruption, stolen personal data, reputational damage and recovery costs.
  5. Prevention: Use MFA, patch systems, maintain offline backups, filter suspicious messages and monitor network activity.
  6. Response: Isolate affected systems, preserve evidence, notify responsible teams and follow incident-response procedures.
Exam tip: If your lecturer expects named recent incidents, use examples specifically covered in your class notes and state the year and impact accurately. The supplied Unit V notes list “recent attacks” as a seminar topic but do not name particular incidents.
24
Explain the role of proxy servers and anonymizers in cybercrime.
Paper 1 · Q13(a)
Definition: A proxy server is an intermediary between a user and a destination server. An anonymizer is a tool or service that attempts to hide identifying details such as the user’s IP address.
  1. The user sends a request to the proxy/anonymizer rather than directly to the destination.
  2. The intermediary forwards the request using its own connection or address.
  3. The destination responds to the intermediary, which passes the response back to the user.
  4. Legitimate uses include privacy, access control, content filtering and caching.
  5. Criminals may misuse them to obscure the source of phishing, scanning or unauthorized access.
  6. High-anonymity proxies attempt to conceal more identifying information than basic proxies.
  7. They do not guarantee complete anonymity; service logs, account data and other evidence may help investigations.
  8. Organizations can use logging, network monitoring and access controls to detect suspicious activity.
Conclusion: Proxies and anonymizers have legitimate privacy uses but can also complicate tracing when misused.

5–7 simple points
B1
What are the main reasons for committing cybercrime?
Paper 1 · Q11(b)
Definition: Reasons for cybercrime are the motives or conditions that encourage a person to commit an illegal activity using digital technology.
  1. Money: Stealing funds, payment details or valuable data.
  2. Curiosity or thrill: Testing systems without permission.
  3. Revenge: Harming a person or organization because of a dispute.
  4. Lack of awareness: Users may fall for scams or misuse technology.
  5. Anonymity: Online identity can be difficult to trace.
  6. Weak security: Poor passwords and outdated systems create opportunities.
  7. Political or business motives: Stealing confidential information or disrupting services.
B2
Define cybercrime and list its key features.
Paper 1 · Q11(a)
Definition: Cybercrime is an illegal act involving a computer, device, network or internet service.
  1. It can target people, property, organizations or governments.
  2. It may be carried out remotely across countries.
  3. It can happen very quickly and affect many victims.
  4. Criminals may attempt to hide their identity.
  5. Evidence is usually digital, such as logs, files and messages.
  6. Technical tools and specialist knowledge may be needed to investigate it.
  7. Examples include phishing, hacking, identity theft and DDoS attacks.
B3
List common methods used in phishing attacks.
Paper 2 · Q11(b)
Definition: Phishing uses fake communications or websites to trick people into revealing private information.
  1. Fake emails and attachments.
  2. SMS messages with harmful links (smishing).
  3. Fraudulent phone calls (vishing).
  4. Copied websites and fake login pages.
  5. Targeted spear-phishing emails.
  6. Fake social-media profiles, advertisements and giveaways.
  7. Prevention: check senders and URLs, do not share OTPs, use MFA and report suspicious messages.
B4
Compare phishing and spear phishing.
Paper 1 · Q13(b)
  1. Phishing: Usually sends similar deceptive messages to many people.
  2. Spear phishing: Targets a particular person or organization.
  3. Information: Traditional phishing may be generic; spear phishing uses personal or workplace details.
  4. Believability: Spear phishing can appear more genuine because it is personalized.
  5. Example: A general fake bank email is phishing; a fake message mentioning a real manager and project is spear phishing.
  6. Safety: Verify unexpected requests using a separate trusted channel.
B5
How can phishing scams be identified?
Paper 1 · Q18(a)
  1. Check the sender’s full email address or phone number.
  2. Look for spelling mistakes, unusual wording and unexpected attachments.
  3. Be cautious about urgent threats, prizes or requests for immediate payment.
  4. Hover over links or inspect the address carefully before opening them.
  5. Do not enter passwords or payment details on links received unexpectedly.
  6. Verify the request through the official app, website or known phone number.
  7. Report the message and delete it after preserving evidence if needed.

What are phishing toolkits?
Unit III notes · supporting phishing questions
Definition: A phishing toolkit is a collection of ready-made tools or templates used to create deceptive messages or fake websites.
  1. It may contain copied login-page templates.
  2. It may automate the sending of deceptive messages.
  3. It may collect information entered by victims.
  4. It can reduce the technical skills needed to run a phishing campaign.
  5. Criminals may use toolkits to imitate trusted services.
  6. Defence includes web filtering, MFA, staff awareness, monitoring and quick reporting.

  2. Attackers may steal confidential files and passwords.
  3. They can allow remote control of a device.
  4. They may be used to install more malware.
  5. They can help attackers remain in a system for a long time.
  6. Organizations may face financial loss, service disruption and privacy damage.

  2. Avoid names, birthdays and common password patterns.
  3. Use a password manager to create and store passwords.
  4. Enable MFA, especially for email and administrator accounts.
  5. Do not click login links in unexpected messages.
  6. Use account lockout or rate limiting where available.
  7. Change exposed passwords and monitor unusual login alerts.

How can cyber security initiatives help prevent recent cybercrime?

  1. Incident response: CERT-In shares alerts and guidance about cyber incidents.
  2. Reporting: Official reporting channels help authorities receive complaints and investigate.
  3. Awareness: Campaigns teach people to identify phishing and fraud.

  5. Coordination: Agencies and organizations can share information and respond faster.

B10
What should be done first to maintain digital evidence integrity?
Paper 2 · Q7 MCQ expanded
  1. Secure and document the scene and affected devices.
  2. Record device condition, date, time and who handled the evidence.
  3. Avoid unnecessary actions that may change data.
  4. Use trained personnel to create forensic images when appropriate.
  5. Calculate and record hashes where suitable to check integrity.
  6. Maintain a chain-of-custody record for every transfer or examination.
